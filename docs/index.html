What it decides, and how.

Nothing to tune

The flags below are what it works out on its own

Everything in the next section can be passed by hand. It no longer has to be. A plain launch — a model and a port, nothing else — notices there is no calibration for this model+GPU+context, runs one on that same port behind a live status page, caches the result, builds the expert atlas from the traffic that calibration itself just generated, and then serves the chat UI on the same port. Every launch after that starts straight into serving.

llama-server -m your-model.gguf --port 8080
# that's it - no -ncmoe, no --moe-cache, no atlas file, no env vars

Substitution — serving a cache miss from a resident stand-in rather than fetching the exact expert — is the largest single lever on a model that does not fit. Measured on the same binary and the same model: rank 2 serves 12.85 tok/s, substitution off 8.3, and with the expert cache off entirely 3.7. It is also the lever that destroys output when pushed too far: rank 0 emits “a question to how a steps explaining on the how quicksort, e shouldicym is” at a very respectable 11.95 tok/s. So candidates are scored for degeneracy (repeated 4-grams, single-word dominance) and rejected outright rather than ranked — speed bought with broken generation cannot win. Rank 0 was rejected exactly this way in the run above. And because those probes are short while the failures show up at 150–250 tokens, every candidate within 15% of the best is re-measured at 4× the probe length, in the configuration actually served, before one is committed.

What this page used to say here was wrong, and the correction is the more useful claim. It read: “the honest limit: on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next this hardware is the binding constraint, not the tuning — 55GB of experts against 30GB of RAM means real swap traffic, and no cache policy removes that.” The swap traffic was real. The conclusion was not. Two of our own bugs were producing it: the loader was page-locking mmap-backed experts on a model larger than RAM, which broke copy-on-write and turned 18.4 GiB of weights into unevictable copies; and every page fault pulled the kernel's 4 MiB sequential read-around for a 0.88 MiB expert slice, so a token read 1.2–1.5 GiB of disk. Fixing both, plus a calibration that could finally reach its own substitution floor, took the same model on the same card from 2.81 to 12.85 tok/s. What remains true: 87 GB does not fit in 12, so it will not reach the 72.62 tok/s a model that fits gets. Blaming the hardware was the comfortable answer, and it stood here unexamined from 4 September until it was measured.

A correction

Three quality gates were rejecting the best configuration

Calibration is supposed to refuse a configuration that is wrong. Three of its checks were refusing configurations that were merely different, and the difference they measured was one the design creates on purpose.

CheckWhat it actually measuredVerdict
Reproducibility
text identity across 5 greedy repeats		 The expert cache admits experts between requests, and an expert computed on GPU and on CPU takes a different quantized path — so identical greedy requests diverge by a word as the cache warms. Cache off: 5 of 5 byte-identical. Cache on: 1 of 5, all five fluent and equivalent. Removed. It fired nine times, every single time at exactly 2-of-5 — sampling noise at n=5 — and never once at the all-distinct signature corruption leaves.
Fidelity bar
similarity to a substitution-free reference		 Substitution serves a different expert by construction, so the wording diverges when the mechanism is working. It rejected ranks 6, 4 and 2, leaving rank 10 at 6.18 tok/s. Demoted to a reported number. Rank 2 scores fidelity 0.00 and answers 4 of 4 verifiable questions correctly. A metric that rates a correct answer at zero cannot gate anything.
Verifiable answers
at 24 tokens		 Our own first attempt at a replacement, and also wrong: on a reasoning model that budget is spent inside the thinking preamble, so the reference itself scored 1 of 4 and every candidate was rejected against that noise. Moved to the confirm step at 320 tokens, where the model can finish thinking before it is scored.

The check that survived is the one that was always right. Degeneracy — repeated 4-grams, single-word dominance — still runs on every candidate, and still rejected rank 0 in the validating run, so removing the other two removed no protection. It is also the only check that ever caught a real bug: the one memory corruption this fork has hit produced deterministic garbage, which an agreement test cannot see by construction.

A second failure mode, found the same day and worth stating because it cuts the other way: a check can also pass something that is broken. Calibration ranks candidates on short probes with reasoning disabled, because that is ~30% cheaper and a short probe then contains a real answer instead of a thinking preamble. The stand-in quality bar was chosen in that regime, passed every probe and the degeneracy guard — and then, served with reasoning on, returned an empty answer with 200 tokens of unterminated reasoning, and a bare </think> for the next prompt. Speed was fine. The failure simply cannot exist in the regime it was measured in. So the cheap probes now only shortlist: anything within 15% of the best is re-measured at full length in the configuration actually served, and that is what decides. Measured necessity — in one run the cheap probe ranked the eventual winner third, and it went on to win the confirm by 54%.

The cost was concrete. Rank 2 — the winner — was rejected three separate ways, and calibration cached something slower in its place. Worse, a rejection was signalled with the same value a crashed server returns, and four stages retry on that, so every wrong rejection was paid for twice; that is what exhausted the time budget and left thread count, cache size and fit margin skipped in milliseconds while being recorded as “failed”. Two of the gemma-4 rejections still listed above — 38.46 and 50.62 tok/s — were this same check. They were slower than that run's winner, so nothing was lost there; that was luck, not the gate working.

What the guard could not see. That degeneracy score is a lexical health check: verbatim 4-gram repeats and single-word dominance. It was also only ever shown one prompt — n_solo_probes is 1, so the whole quality verdict rested on probe_prompts[0], a long substantial instruction. Long prompts are the easy case: the model has a paragraph of task anchoring every next token. Two gaps closed since: a short-prompt pass ("hi", "thanks!", "What's 2 + 2?") scored for quality but never for speed, and an output-fidelity check that compares each substitution rung against a substitution-free reference — with the tolerance measured from the model's own run-to-run resampling rather than written down as a constant.

What the ladder decides now. Beyond placement, threads, cache size and fit margin: the substitution floor, and — new — the stand-in quality bar, which is the actual wait-or-substitute balance. A miss can either wait for the real expert over PCIe or run immediately on a resident one, and the bar decides which. Measured on gemma-4 at the two extremes: always wait gives 32.66 tok/s with correct output; never wait gives 51.07 tok/s and the model emits nothing but * * * *. That is why the bar is searched against the fidelity and reproducibility gates rather than set to whatever is fastest.

The stand-in itself is chosen by co-activation now, not by heat. Ranking by heat picked the most popular resident expert rather than one that had ever done the same job, which is the mechanism behind the quality collapse substitution used to be blamed for. The eviction scan window and protected-segment cap stopped being compile-time constants for the same reason, and the Brain view's edge count is cut at the largest drop in edge strength per layer instead of at a fixed number — the same eigengap rule the atlas already uses to choose how many axes it wants.

Everything on, one command

Run it the way it was measured

Every feature on this page, enabled together. This is the exact command the numbers below were taken from — a 26B MoE model at 64k context on a single 12GB RTX 3060. Most of it is now optional: see the section above for what a bare llama-server -m model.gguf works out on its own. This form is what you write when you want to pin a specific value rather than let it be measured.

llama-server -m gemma-4-26B-A4B-it-UD-Q4_K_M.gguf

That is the whole command. On a first run it calibrates itself and serves a progress page on the port until it is done; afterwards it reads what it measured. From that one line it works out the expert placement for the context you asked for, sizes the expert cache, picks the thread count and fit margin, finds the MTP draft model sitting beside the target and turns on speculative decoding, keeps a per-model co-activation graph and regenerates the atlas from it every few minutes, and enables the expert cache, substitution, neuron-reduce and atlas lookahead. Everything below is what those defaults resolve to — pass a flag only to pin a value rather than let it be measured.

It also refuses to use its own stale measurements. Calibration entries carry the version of the gates they were recorded under. An entry written before the output-fidelity and reproducibility checks existed was never tested by them, so its substitution settings are ignored with the reason printed, while the placement and sizing from the same entry — which those gates never had an opinion about — still apply. This is not hypothetical: a gemma-4 entry recorded rank 2 because it was fastest, and the fidelity check added later scored that same rank at 0.33.

The first few requests after startup run slower while the expert cache is still filling; the steady-state number is the one to plan around.

Two optional extras, deliberately not in the line above. --cache-disk DIR persists the prompt cache so a restart costs 271ms instead of 5.8s — but it writes prompt content to disk (owner-only, model-fingerprinted), so it is opt-in on purpose. And the router-bias alternative to substitution above, LLAMA_MOE_CACHE_BIAS=0.5, for anyone who wants to try it ahead of a default decision.

Why this exists

Capable inference on hardware people actually own

Running a genuinely large model used to mean a rack of enterprise GPUs, or accepting whatever a hosted API gives you. The core unlock here — a GPU-resident cache for CPU-offloaded MoE experts — means a 26B-parameter MoE model that can't fit in a 12GB card doesn't just technically run: it runs fast, on a single consumer GPU. That's the whole point: put capability that used to require a data center within reach of hardware someone actually has on their desk.

But a fork that just claims speedups is exactly as trustworthy as any other unverified benchmark. So the second half of the project is making sure every claim can be checked: numbers are measured before/after on real hardware and reported honestly even when they're a loss (see the FR-Spec row below), and the cache itself is watchable live — not "trust me, it's caching," but a real-time view of which experts are hot right now. Prove it, then ship it, then let you go verify it yourself. Not a fork chasing every upstream feature — one willing to slow down and prove each claim before it ships.

Scope, honestly: the expert-cache fits the model's parameters into limited VRAM - it doesn't and can't stretch VRAM to hold arbitrary context length, which is a separate resource that scales with sequence length regardless of how the weights are cached. Pushed this to a real boundary on our own 12GB card: requesting 1M context used to hard-crash the whole process rather than fail gracefully - found and fixed that (--fit now searches by doubling from a safe floor and lands on a real, measured context size instead of guessing or crashing). A physical ceiling still exists - ask for 1M on a 12GB card and you will not get 1M - but it sits much higher than we first reported, and finding out why was the more useful result.

The first version of that fix asked the wrong question. It picked a placement, then shrank context until the model fit around it - so a 12GB card landed on 8192 tokens/slot, and we wrote that down as the hardware ceiling. It wasn't. The expert placement had been fixed first (by a command-line flag nobody revisited), and context was quietly paying for it. Inverting the order - decide placement from the context actually asked for, pushing more expert weights to CPU RAM until the request fits - gives 65,536 tokens across 4 concurrent slots on that same card and model, 8x the context and 4x the concurrency, at higher throughput than before. Nothing about the hardware changed; the program simply stopped trading away the one thing the user explicitly asked for. The lesson we'd pass on: when two automatic systems each optimise locally, check what the pair does to the number the user actually cares about.

Context that follows the request, not the flag. Placement used to be chosen first and context shrunk to fit around it; now the requested context is fixed and expert placement gives way to it. Same hardware, 16× the context and 4× the concurrency.

Restarts stop costing a re-read. The prompt cache now persists to disk keyed by content hash, so a 3,600-token document that cost 5.8s to process is restored in 0.27s after a full restart. Files are owner-only and model-fingerprinted - they contain your prompts, and the docs say so plainly.

KV cache type is not a flat win either way - it depends on how tight VRAM already is. Symmetric q8_0 halves the conversation cache, and when the freed memory is what lets a draft model load at all (Qwen3.8-Flash-Next at 64k, below) it is a clear win: +9.7% and the difference between serving with a draft or not. On this card, with this model, at this same 64k context - where VRAM is comfortable rather than desperate - the paired, controlled result reverses: f16 KV measured 3.5-4% faster than q8_0, because the freed cache slots did not translate into enough extra hit rate to beat the plainer compute path. Both numbers are real; which one applies depends on whether the model you are running is VRAM-starved or not. Full needle recall at 10/50/90% depth of a 24,700-token prompt either way - the quantization is not lossy for retrieval at this context length, whichever speed wins for your model.

And the ones that didn't work, because a fork that only publishes its wins is the thing this project exists not to be: pinning hot experts in RAM measured worse than doing nothing and ships disabled; page-cache readahead gained 5% on the first request and cost 8% thereafter, so it ships disabled too; and a claimed “+21% to +50%” speedup was retracted outright once measured properly - it was a single noisy sample, and the real effect is about 5%. The benchmark harness that caught it is in the repository.

What this is

A fork that measures its own claims

solid.cpp tracks llama.cpp upstream directly and layers on a curated set of performance and correctness fixes — triaged from real, reported issues, root-caused in code, and validated with actual measurements before anything is kept. Every change here is either a measured win or a documented, honest "no": a fix that was tried, found not to be the cause, and kept anyway if it was independently valid, or reverted if it wasn't.

The starting focus was MoE expert-cache placement and speculative decoding, but the scope covers the whole inference hot path — kernels, batching, KV-cache, sampling, quantization — and, increasingly, whether you can actually see what any of it is doing while it runs.

Live, not just logged

The Brain view: watch the cache, don't just trust it

A cache you can't see into is a black box you have to take on faith. The Brain view polls the running server and renders, live, which experts are actually resident in GPU memory right now — cold (never cached), warm (probation), or hot (protected) — with real-time hit flashes as tokens route through them.

What building this actually caught: live-testing the Brain view against real traffic surfaced a genuine, previously-undiscovered engine bug — under backend sampling (-bs), the entire moe-cache session was silently destroyed and rebuilt empty on every request, because its lifetime was tied to a scheduler object that gets torn down and recreated whenever a new sampler is attached. The cache was working for exactly one request, then quietly doing nothing, with no error or warning. Root-caused with temporary per-call logging (not guessed at), fixed by detaching the cache session from the old scheduler and re-attaching it to the replacement instead of recreating it from scratch. This is exactly why the "proven, not promised" numbers above matter — a feature that silently stops working is worse than one that was never built, because you don't find out until you go looking.

Where experts live, measured not assumed

The Atlas — a good map, and proof the system can't tell

Every expert gets a position from measured topic affinity: probed offline across nine categories with greedy decode and a replication gate, so one lucky prompt can't promote an expert to specialist. The map is genuinely predictive. The finding that matters is that being predictive doesn't reach the running system at all.

As a map, it holds up. Tested on link prediction — “do these two experts fire together?” — against embeddings factored directly out of the observed co-activation graph, on 31.6× more data than the atlas ever saw:

Then the same two maps were run through the live server, and were indistinguishable. Probe atlas (AUC 0.7116): 58.45 tok/s, 71.20% hit. The map's advantage is real as a statement about maps and never reaches the mechanism consuming it. Any claim that one atlas beats another now has to say which of the two things it means.

That 2% figure is from the original design; a later rebuild (free-slot-only admission, a wider candidate pool, burst-mode firing on a detected topic shift) touches roughly 22% of decisions instead, and fixed two designs measured actively harmful (−0.31pp, −6.55pp) along the way. Current honest state: safe, not harmful, but still short of a consistent win — post-switch pooled hit-rate delta of −0.35pp overall, with individual topics mixed rather than uniform (one, law, is a real +0.40pp win, 3 of 4 rounds). Ships off by default either way.

Why the ceiling is that low is the deeper answer: atlas warming gains +0.31pp hit rate in simulation, while a topic oracle with perfect foreknowledge of the segment's experts gains +0.30pp. The atlas is already at the ceiling. Every topic touches ~6,000 of 7,680 experts and the top 1,000 cover only 52–61% of selections — there is no compact topic hot set to preload, so no prefetcher of this shape can win. One result explaining an entire family of refuted experiments is worth more than the feature would have been.

So the atlas ships as what it demonstrably is: the spatial layer of the Brain view — position from measurement, colour from live cache state — with its warming path rate-limited, admission-gated and off by default. Related reversals were reported the same way: combining atlas admission with eviction-weighting went net negative (−0.95pp, 1 of 4 rounds) against admission alone (+2.75pp, 4 of 4); and hit rate itself turned out to be purchasable — 79.25% is available for one environment variable and a slower server, which is exactly why throughput, not hit rate, is the objective here.

The calibration was optimising a machine that does not exist

Every number was measured at 4k while the run asked for 64k

Calibration launches real servers and benchmarks them, which is the whole point. Its candidates ran with --fit enabled, so each one's own fit logic was free to trade the requested context down until the rest of its configuration fitted — and it did, silently.

A run launched with -c 65536 produced candidate after candidate reporting n_ctx_slot = 4096. Every value that run chose was chosen at 4k. That is not a small discrepancy, because VRAM is the binding constraint and context consumes most of it: at 4k there is room to spare, so the sweeps picked a 2048 MiB expert cache and -ub 2048 — and both of them OOM at 65536 with the draft attached, 676 MiB short. The run optimised a configuration the machine cannot serve, and nothing in it could tell, because a candidate that quietly shrank its context still returned a perfectly good tok/s for a configuration nobody had asked about.

The pattern, and the rule that came out of it: a knob measured on a narrow metric can invalidate every stage beneath it, and nothing detects that. So the search is now ordered by what each knob does rather than by convenience: knobs that change the fixed cost (context, KV precision, micro-batch, placement, draft width) are settled first and the remainder is then measured with the model loaded; knobs that divide that remainder (cache size, the reduced pool, the draft's share, the ring) are searched against the real number; and knobs that spend no bytes (admission, substitution, eviction, the prerouter's influence) come last, where they cannot invalidate anything above them. The expert-cache ladder used to derive its ceiling from an idle card — which is how an 8192 MiB rung became a candidate on a 12 GiB card, and how it won by 0.26% over 2048 while the pool allocator silently halved it to whatever fitted.

q4_0 is excluded by policy rather than measured and rejected. This is a reasoning model, the KV cache holds the whole chain of thought, and 4-bit K and V degrade it in a way the answer gate is not guaranteed to catch — a reply can stay fluent and verifiable while the reasoning behind it has drifted. A win q4_0 produced would be a win we should not take.

And the draft's own KV stays f16, deliberately. The asymmetry is the point: the target is 48 layers against the draft's single NextN block, so q8_0 on the target frees a gigabyte while q8_0 on the draft would free a rounding error — and the target's degradation is the kind the answer gate can see, because it checks what the model answers. The draft's cannot be seen that way. Acceptance is the draft's entire value and it is measured in agreement with the target, not in output anyone reads: 0.97 acceptance pays for itself, 0.43 costs more than it returns. A quantised KV degrades exactly that agreement, since the head predicts from state it then has to agree with. Quantise the large consumer whose output is checked; leave the small one whose worth is measured in how often it is right.

A predictor that trains itself while it serves

The prerouter: learning which experts are coming, one layer ahead

Status, 2 October 2026: the clean win described below (71.41 vs 50.16 tok/s, "on both models") was measured in the calibration probe regime - a small cache under heavy eviction pressure. Re-tested on the shipped stack with a larger cache (0.84-0.86 hit rate, not contended): gemma-4 measures a tie with the pre-router frozen, and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next measures -7% with it on - though that Qwen run did not confirm the saved predictor weights actually loaded, so it may be testing an untrained model, not a trained one. Kept on by default anyway (no measured harm on gemma, and the Qwen result needs a clean re-run before acting on it either way), but "wins on both models" below is the calibration-probe story, not the shipped one.

The expert cache decides what to keep. A prerouter decides what is about to be wanted — a small logistic model over the hidden state, trained by SGD on every real routing decision the model makes, with labels that cost nothing because the router produces them a layer later.

It was already in the tree, and it had never once been switched on. TRAIN_PREDICTOR defaulted to 0 and appeared nowhere in the calibration path, so every run this fork had ever done served with it off — the same opt-in trap that had already hidden the substitution floor. Making it a measured stage is what produced every number below, and three of them changed the design.

Trained same-layer, it is not a prerouter at all. It learns which experts this layer wants from an input the real router is about to answer exactly — so the score can only ever rank experts outside the current selection. Pairing the previous layer's features with this layer's decision asks a question nothing else has answered yet, and that is the only version prefetch can spend. The pairing crosses a real layer boundary only: one logical layer dispatches several expert tensors off a single routing decision, and without that check most “transitions” are a layer paired with itself — measured at 45 of 64 exported cross-layer edges.

The architecture's own n-gram table turned out to be the most valuable input in the run. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next gathers its per-layer n-gram embedding once, before the layer loop, and injects a slice into every layer's residual — so it is a component of the next layer's input that is known exactly, now, while the hidden state is stale by one layer's residual. That staleness is precisely what caps router lookahead at 59.3%. Removing the n-gram block costs more than any other lever measured here: 70.00 → 49.88 tok/s.

On a model whose working set is largely resident, the prerouter pays off as a retention policy, not a prefetcher — there is little to fetch but plenty worth not losing. That result is consistent with the prediction ring measuring best at 0% in the same run: the ring exists to give admissions somewhere to land, and admission is the consumer that lost. Whether this inverts on a model that does not fit — where a miss costs a real NVMe fetch — is the open question, and is being measured on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next now.

The measurement was nearly worthless, twice, and both times for the same reason. The predictor admits through a suggestion-only path whose own comment records that pool.free_slots “empties permanently within the first few tokens of any real run” — so every prediction was being discarded for want of a slot, and the stage would have reported that the predictor does not help about a mechanism that was never allowed to act. Giving that path eviction rights is not the fix: that was built, measured worse (−6.55pp), and reverted. The prediction ring is the middle path, where a prediction displaces only an older prediction and never a resident expert. Separately, the on/off arms had to carry the same ring as each other — the ring moves throughput on its own, so an A/B where only the “on” arm has one measures the ring, not the predictor.

Two predictors, and the one that can be late is not the one that admits

There are two mechanisms predicting into the same ring, and they were competing for its slots first-come-first-served — the rotation was FIFO, so it went to whichever fired more often rather than whichever was more often right. They do not fire alike:

Admission has to be on time — a fetch landing after the layer ran is pure waste. Retention does not: a slightly stale “this will be wanted” is still exactly what you need to decide what not to throw away. The droppable mechanism lost the punctual job and won the one that tolerates being late. So the roles are assigned rather than searched — lookahead admits, the prerouter retains — and the calibration stage now measures the role's strength, keeping an inert floor and an “admission too” arm so the assignment stays falsifiable.

Substitution is deliberately not assigned, because the two models genuinely disagree, and explicably so: a stand-in is a resemblance question, and on a model that mostly fits there is little to gain while a wrong stand-in costs quality — whereas on one where 48 expert layers live on CPU nearly every miss needs a stand-in and the extra signal earns its place.

And where both mechanisms want the same expert, that is now its own signal rather than a collision. The second one to arrive does not need a slot; it needs its agreement recorded — the slot is promoted above what either mechanism could claim alone. This file already made that argument for cross-depth agreement: two independent samples landing on the same expert “should be much rarer and much more often right than either depth alone”.

Stated plainly: this is not yet an end-to-end win. Every figure above is the calibration probe regime, and this hardware shows ±10% run-to-run variance on identical configurations (ncmoe=21 measured 68.39 and 61.47 tok/s an hour apart). The three comparisons point the same way, which is what makes them worth reporting — but the full calibrated combination validates at 58.94 tok/s, and no like-for-like run of that same combination without the predictor has been measured yet. Until that exists, the honest claim is that the mechanism works and the direction is consistent, not that throughput improved.

Below the expert: individual neurons

Sparse activation: not every neuron in a hot expert earns its VRAM

Caching decides which experts live in GPU memory. But an expert is not atomic — inside one, some neurons carry almost all the signal and others contribute essentially nothing. This fork measures that directly, per expert, and serves the ones that matter at reduced width.

Status, 1 October 2026: this mechanism is no longer on by default. It measured +8% on a model that does not fit in memory, but shipped on for every model with one fixed width (256 neurons), which on gemma-4 is 18% of an expert and cost about a third of the throughput (switching it off: +50%, faster in all 24 paired requests; see the audit above). It is on only where a calibration measures it as a win.

The measurement came first, and it changed the design twice. Concentration is scored by value, not rank: “top 25% of neurons” picks a fixed cutoff regardless of what that expert's distribution actually looks like, so instead each neuron is compared against a threshold relative to its own expert's maximum, and the resulting count falls out as an observation rather than an input. Dead-neuron fraction is anchored on the median, not the mean — these distributions are skewed enough (max/mean ratios of 120–270× measured) that the mean is not a trustworthy reference.

Converted experts are served from a second, narrower cache pool: gate/up only (down_exps's row axis is an output dimension, where dropping rows would delete real outputs rather than approximate sparsity), the chosen rows gathered once at fill time, and a small CUDA kernel scattering the computed values back into their correct positions in an otherwise-zeroed row. The CPU path never learns any of this exists. Measured 50% VRAM saved on every converted expert, and the reduced pool evicts under the same heat-driven LFRU policy as the primary one, so it keeps tracking whichever experts are currently hot instead of freezing at whoever converted first.

What building this actually caught — three times, and only by measuring: every one of these looked correct on inspection. (1) Taking the cache's core lock on every hit cost 16% throughput (35.56 → 29.89 tok/s) — the exact regression shape an earlier dedicated mutex already existed to prevent, reintroduced. (2) A full session decided 20,201 conversions and placed zero of them: greedy pool admission consumed the entire budget first, so the reduced pool never had room to exist — pure overhead, no benefit, completely silent. (3) Reduction was being applied to down_exps, which the design explicitly excludes; the restriction had been written down but never coded. The fix for (2) then failed twice more on its own, each time visible only in the pool table: reserving a flat slice starved a whole primary pool out of existence and dropped hit rate 31.3% → 23.1%, costing far more than it saved.

Where the throughput actually was

1 October 2026 — an audit of served speed

Looking for a regression, and measuring each candidate before believing it

Serving gemma-4-26B at -ncmoe 99 --moe-cache auto --parallel 2 -c 65536, decode read about 33 tok/s on a mixed prompt set against the 70.9 recorded by the 13 September calibration. The gap had three parts: the workload (prose is accepted by the draft ~38% of the time, code and reasoning 73–90%, and the calibration probed only the latter two), two defaults that were costing real speed, and measurement that could not have found them. This section is what was measured, in the order it was understood.

How it was measured

Every setting receives the same ten prompts with the same seeds (request i is the identical request in every setting) and the comparison is request against request, not median against median: single-request spread is ±8–12 tok/s, so unpaired medians hide any effect under ~15%. Each start serves two timed cold requests (the pool still filling), one untimed, then eight timed warm requests; the whole list repeats for three rounds, interleaved. The cache's own summary at shutdown gives the slot count and hit rate. The baseline is the previous defaults (neuron subsetting on, draft depth 8, no confidence gate); it read 29.0 / 33.5 / 31.3 tok/s by round, which is the noise floor the table has to clear. Slot count varies from run to run (2,644–3,197 here, set by how much VRAM is free at the cache's first fill) and drives hit rate, so hit rates are only comparable at equal slots.

The two biggest effects have a cause. Neuron subsetting shipped on for every model with a fixed width of 256 neurons per expert — 18% of gemma-4's 1,408 — where the model's own coverage curve says a median 83% is needed to hold 90% of the activation mass; it cost about a third of the throughput and bought no cache gain. Part of that cost was mechanical: the reduced-expert dispatch compacted its activations with one device copy per reduced row (about 30 per MoE op, 60 ops per token, on the decode thread), a loop the original comment already named as the thing to remove; it is one kernel now. The draft depth was the other: the calibration that picked depth 8 probed code and reasoning only, and on prose a shallower draft keeps the verify batch inside the GPU's 5-wide MoE fast path.

What the earlier tests missed, and why

A first round switched each off-by-default flag on, untrained, with different prompts per setting, and found most of them tied or lost. One of those verdicts was overturned on paired prompts (p_min), and lazy KV's cache gain did show up there — though it later turned out to cost long-prompt stability and was made opt-in again. The causes of the misses matter:

  • No identical-pair control. An unpaired test read -ncmoe 30 and -ncmoe 99 20% apart — but gemma-4 has 30 layers, so they are the same placement. Any comparison under ~20% needs an identical copy of the baseline in the same run to show the noise floor.
  • Unpaired prompts. With ±8–12 tok/s between requests, eight different prompts per setting cannot resolve a 10–15% effect.
  • Warming features can only fill free slots. Atlas warming, predictor-driven warming and lookahead admission place a predicted expert only into a free slot, only while more than half the pool is free, and never evict (an evicting variant measured −6.55 points). On gemma-4 at 64k the pool is full within the first requests, so on a warm cache they have nowhere to put anything; their documented wins were cold-start gains, mostly in hit rate (0.1–1.1 points) on a different model. The paired test times the first requests after start separately for exactly this reason — and still finds no gain for atlas warming.
  • The trained pre-router did not reproduce its documented win here. Its gemma-4 gain (50.2 → 60.9 tok/s, 59.2 → 70.0 predicting the next layer) was measured in the calibration's probe regime, with a small 2,048 MiB cache under heavy eviction pressure. Trained on 60 requests and run frozen, it measures within noise of the baseline on this larger cache; learning while it serves costs a few percent. Whether it earns a place depends on the cache being contended, which the larger cache here is not.

Where this session stopped, and why

HOBBIT ranks the router's chosen experts by cumulative gate weight and skips the ones below a mass threshold entirely, rather than substituting them — a more faithful idea than gating by rank position alone, which is what the shipped substitution floor does. Reading the dispatch code to scope it found that the planner's own notion of "rank" is purely positional (which slot in the top-k list), with no gate-weight value reaching it at all: building this properly means threading a weights array through the op callback that every MoE layer's dispatch goes through, not adding a flag. That is a change to code every model exercises on every token, and it was not rushed in at the end of a long session. Scoped and left for a clean start instead.

Two measurement traps found on the way, both of which produced confident wrong answers. A benchmark that sends raw prompts without the chat template makes gemma's instruct model degenerate in every configuration — including with the cache off — which read exactly like the cache corrupting output. And timing prose against a number calibrated on code makes a healthy configuration look half as fast. The first was reported as a bug and retracted the same hour.

Real-time per-expert tier/heat heatmap merged with measured topic-affinity positioning, served straight off the running server — see above.

Expert-prefetch + host-pinning

Overlaps an offloaded expert's weight upload with the previous expert's compute, plus lifetime host-page pinning for -ncmoe's CPU→GPU copies. +36.0% prefill — ported from thecodacus.

LFRU-aware prefill hit/miss split

Prefetch now checks per-expert residency against the cache decode already fills: a resident expert copies device-to-device instead of re-fetching from host memory. Design from FreeToken, re-derived against our own cache — zero regression on a cold cache.

Live NVMe-prefetch hint for cold experts (GGML_CUDA_MOE_CACHE_LIVE_PREFETCH, default on)

The live-activation predictor already ranked candidates and the cache already tracked per-expert CPU cold/warm state — admission just never acted on it. Now hints the kernel to start reading a cold candidate's host pages the moment it's queued, not when the fill worker actually needs them. A/B'd under real memory pressure (5 rounds each, 15 GiB cap): 29.1 → 39.8 tok/s mean, and it specifically kills the worst-case stalls (17 tok/s low end gone) rather than just nudging the average — unlike the older one-shot READAHEAD flag (blind, history-based, a measured net loss on warm throughput), this only fires for a candidate already independently wanted.

Cost-aware (GreedyDual-Size) eviction tiebreak

Eviction now weighs heat by an NVMe-vs-RAM cost tier, so a hotter-but-cheap slot can lose to a colder-but-expensive one. A correctness fix for real memory pressure, not a throughput win — noise-neutral under normal load.

Cross-depth-agreement speculative eviction (SPEC_EVICT_MODE=agree, default)

Only lets a closer, more accurate depth's prediction confirm a farther depth's guess before evicting — fixes the regression the old any policy had at depth≥3. Default now, inert unless GGML_CUDA_MOE_LOOKAHEAD_DEPTH>=2 is set.

Probabilistic draft acceptance

--spec-prob-accept accepts a draft token whenever the target considers it at least as likely as the draft did, not only on exact match. On by default since 1 October 2026 (turning it off measured −9%, see the audit above); verified byte-identical when off, and exact under greedy sampling — at temperature above 0 it biases the output very slightly toward the draft, and --no-spec-prob-accept restores exact-match sampling.

FR-Spec-style vocab trimming

MTP draft-vocab trimming with its real cost measured directly — a genuine trade of throughput for VRAM, not a free win, plus a cache-correctness bug found and fixed along the way.

p_min footgun warning

A diagnostic that fires when a drafter's confidence early-stop is left disabled while draft width has been raised — mirrors the existing concurrency-cliff warning pattern.

Permanent debug instrumentation

LLAMA_DEBUG_VERIFY=1 gates zero-cost-when-unset traces that were directly responsible for finding more than one of the fixes on this page.

Context-size fitting doesn't hard-crash

An explicit -c request too large for VRAM used to abort the whole process - upstream's --fit only auto-adjusts unset arguments. Now searches by doubling from a safe floor and lands on the real, measured context that fits instead - see above for how this one was actually found.

Stale LFRU hit-mask corruption, root-caused

A garbled, repeated-character output bug, git-bisected and root-caused: the device-to-device hit bitmask is legitimately shared across gate_exps/up_exps/down_exps, but LFRU residency is tracked per tensor — a bit cleared for one left the others neither device- nor host-copied, reading garbage as weights. Fixed by separating the shared routing bitmask from a fresh per-tensor copy mask.

Recurrent-state dtype: -ctr/-cts

Gated DeltaNet/KDA's recurrent state was hardcoded F32 with no override, unlike K/V cache's -ctk/-ctv. New flags mirror that pattern; f16 halves the footprint (62.81 → 31.41 MiB) with output verified coherent — needed a new F16 ggml_cuda_op_scale kernel underneath it too.

Live-activation predictor for expert-cache warming

A small linear layer trained online on the real hidden state each routing decision came from, feeding the same free-slot-only admission path the topic atlas uses. Self-tuning throughout, weights persisted across restarts. 40–45% top-1 accuracy from a cold start (~3% chance); +0.12pp hit rate, 3/4 rounds — the first mechanism here to beat the no-warming baseline on repeat measurement.

Description

Inherited from upstream, unchanged

The core goal of llama.cpp — minimal-setup, state-of-the-art LLM/VLM inference on a wide range of hardware, locally and in the cloud — carries through untouched:

  • Plain C/C++ implementation without any dependencies
  • Apple silicon as a first-class citizen — ARM NEON, Accelerate and Metal
  • AVX, AVX2, AVX512 and AMX for x86; RVV/ZVFH/ZFH/ZICBOP/ZIHINTPAUSE for RISC-V
  • 1.5-bit through 8-bit integer quantization for faster inference and reduced memory use
  • Custom CUDA kernels for NVIDIA GPUs, plus AMD (HIP) and Moore Threads (MUSA) support
  • Vulkan and SYCL backends; CPU+GPU hybrid inference for models bigger than VRAM
  • Newest speculative-decoding drafters — --spec-type draft-dflash (DFlash, block-diffusion parallel drafting, up to 6× lossless speedup) and --spec-type draft-dspark (DSpark, DeepSeek's confidence-scheduled semi-autoregressive Markov-head drafter, 16–18% higher accepted length than DFlash) — full convert-script and README coverage in docs/speculative.md. Not something this fork ported: real, working, upstream-inherited support, checked and confirmed present rather than assumed. DFlare (scales draft capacity further past DFlash) is a newer successor worth watching, not yet implemented anywhere.

Built on top of ggml. See upstream's own manifesto and API references for lib llama and llama-server REST.

Real sources for real ported work, not vendored libraries but genuine prior art this fork builds directly on:

  • leloch/llama.cpp (moe-cache-pr branch) — original design for the GPU-resident MoE expert-cache this whole fork is built around. The core feature, not a footnote.
  • thecodacus/llama.cpp (fable5/prefetch-experts branch, via the Codacus YouTube channel) — expert-prefetch + lifetime host-pinning, overlapping expert-weight uploads with compute instead of blocking on them. Cherry-picked and re-measured on our own hardware: +36.0% prefill (1403 → 1908 tok/s, RTX 3060, Gemma-4-26B-A4B).
  • JustVugg/colibri — LFRU eviction design (probation/protected segments, heat-based promotion) and the Expert Atlas topic-affinity measurement methodology (labeled probes, replication gate, specialization scoring) the Brain/Atlas view's positioning is built on. Its two-step shared-expert router prediction experiment also saved us building a predictor: it reports the best recall in this space (76.7%, beating PILOT) and no end-to-end gain, because cache capacity bound first — a result we confirmed applies to us before writing any code.
  • FlashML-org/FreeToken — the hit/miss split for MoE prefill weight uploads (device-to-device gather for experts already cache-resident, one batched transfer for the rest, instead of always paying full PCIe cost). Design taken, not code: FreeToken's own version runs as a from-scratch engine with a single unified expert cache, so we re-derived it against our existing LFRU pool and the native sparse-copy path in ggml_backend_sched_compute_splits rather than porting its CUDA kernels directly — the residency check and device-to-device copy are ours, keyed off the same cache the decode path already fills.

Prior art that shaped decisions here without code being taken — including several that told us not to build something:

  • antirez/ds4 (DwarfStar) — the three-tier design this fork independently arrived at (resident non-routed weights, a budgeted routed-expert cache, GGUF on SSD for misses), plus the explicit read/write buffer approach we tested against our mmap-based one. Reviewing it corrected two of our assumptions and sized the remaining gap honestly.
  • vLLM — PagedAttention. We deliberately did not port it: block tables and gather kernels would mean new attention kernels for every backend llama.cpp supports. Instead we took its actual saving — don't commit memory for context nobody uses — via CUDA's virtual-memory API, which keeps addresses contiguous so existing kernels are untouched. Its automatic prefix caching turned out to already exist upstream here.
  • Speculating Experts, Cross-Layer Gate / PILOT / Fate, FineMoE, SP-MoE — the expert-prefetch literature, which established that the field solves this by predicting and prefetching in time rather than holding experts resident, and that realistic gains are ~14% rather than the order of magnitude we had been chasing. That correction is why three of our residency experiments ship disabled.
  • Checked the literature's two standard explanations for why holding hot experts resident measures worse than doing nothing (cross-NUMA-node remote access; missing huge-page backing causing page-fault storms — up to ~1M faults loading one 4GB expert at 4KiB pages) against this specific machine before accepting either as the cause: it has one NUMA node (no cross-socket penalty possible) and transparent_hugepage=always already covers a plain malloc buffer without code changes. Both explanations are ruled out here, not confirmed by assumption — the negative result stands for a different, still-undetermined reason.
  • MoE-Lightning and MoE-Lens — throughput-oriented MoE serving under GPU memory constraints; LMCache-style three-tier KV hierarchies (hot on GPU, warm on host DRAM, cold on NVMe) are the closest published analogue to this fork's own NVMe-migration result and GreedyDual-Size cost-tier eviction, though those target KV-cache blocks, not expert weights — the natural next step if expert-weight NVMe residency is ever built as a real tier rather than left to the OS page cache.
